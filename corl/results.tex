\subsection{Hardware Comparison}
\label{subsec:results-hardware-comparison}

We evaluate GaitNet on a physical Unitree Go1 against the
\textit{Legged Perceptive Stack} \cite{leggedcontrol}, a
state-of-the-art open-source framework combining Nonlinear Model
Predictive Control (NMPC) and Whole-Body Control (WBC) atop the OCS2
solver, using rigidly scheduled phase-optimized trajectories. On
hardware, GaitNet supplies footstep targets directly to this same
NMPC/WBC pipeline in place of its native gait scheduler
(\autoref{subsec:methodology-system-overview}), so the comparison
below holds the low-level tracking controller fixed and isolates the
contribution of the planning layer alone.

A physical test track (\autoref{fig:figure-test-environment}) mirrors
the terrain used in simulation: narrow strips of rigid wooden ground
interspersed with gaps of varying, pseudo-randomly distributed width.
We characterize a track's difficulty as the percentage of its area
that \emph{cannot} be stepped on, ranging from $\corlDifficultyMin\%$
(flat ground) to $\corlDifficultyMax\%$ (dense gaps). Because both
conditions share a controller, tracking error would mostly reflect the
shared low-level pipeline rather than the planner; we instead measure
mission-level autonomy directly: success rate (completing the
traversal without the robot's base contacting the ground or drifting
off the track), total steps taken, and traversal time, each over
\corlHwTrialsPerCell{} independent physical trials per method and
difficulty level.

We compare four conditions, isolating the contribution of emergent
concurrency directly:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Legged Perceptive Stack:} the NMPC/WBC baseline
    described above \cite{leggedcontrol}.
  \item \textbf{GaitNet-Single:} GaitNet with the contact-count
    constraint tightened to permit only one leg in swing at a time,
    otherwise identical to the full model.
  \item \textbf{GaitNet-Trot:} GaitNet restricted to only swinging
    diagonally opposing leg pairs concurrently, matching a
    conventional trot's phase structure.
  \item \textbf{GaitNet (Full):} the proposed planner under its
    standard constraint of at most two concurrent swing legs, with no
    restriction on \emph{which} legs those are.
\end{enumerate}
A trial is successful only if the robot completes the full traversal
without falling or placing a foot in a removed terrain section.

GaitNet (Full) achieves the highest success rate of the four methods,
with the gap widening substantially at high difficulty
($\corlHighDifficultyRangeLow$--$\corlDifficultyMax\%$):
the Legged Perceptive Stack's fixed phase schedule, and the
GaitNet-Trot and GaitNet-Single ablations, all increasingly commit to
footholds inside a scheduled or single-leg sequence that cannot be
revised once the terrain beneath it turns out to be invalid, whereas
GaitNet (Full) re-evaluates every leg independently at every 40\,ms
tick and can commit a second leg mid-swing to recover from a
foothold the first leg is already unable to use productively. This
matches the ordering predicted by
\autoref{subsec:methodology-high-rate-selection}: GaitNet-Single, the
most constrained ablation, performs closest to the scheduled baseline,
GaitNet-Trot recovers some concurrency and some performance, and
GaitNet (Full) is uniformly best.

\subsection{Simulation Results}
\label{subsec:results-simulation}

Prior to hardware deployment, we evaluate the same four conditions in
NVIDIA Isaac Lab across a grid of terrain difficulties
($\corlDifficultyMin\%$ to $\corlDifficultyMax\%$, in
$\corlDifficultyStep\%$ increments) and commanded forward velocities
($\corlVelocityMin$ to $\corlVelocityMax$\,m/s), over
\corlSimTrialsPerCell{} trials of \corlSimEpisodeDuration\,s each per
cell, following the protocol of our prior simulation study
\cite{Sullivan2025}. The simulation results reported here were
collected under the candidate-sampling scheme described in
\autoref{subsec:methodology-gaitnet-training}
(\corlTrainCandidatesPerLeg{} randomly sampled candidates per leg,
cost-ablated); we are re-collecting the same grid under the deployed
refinement procedure
(\autoref{subsec:methodology-gaitnet-gradient-ascent}) to confirm the
numbers carry over, and note this explicitly rather than presenting
figures from one pipeline as validation of the other.

Under this configuration, GaitNet (Full) achieves a
$\corlResCostAblatedSurvivalRate\%$ mean survival rate, compared to
$\corlResBaselineSurvivalRate\%$ for an independent single-leg
baseline planner: a lowest-cost footstep selector operating one leg
at a time with a fixed $\corlBaselineSwingDurationMs$\,ms swing
duration, structurally identical to the GaitNet-Single ablation but
without a learned value function. GaitNet maintains strong
performance for commanded velocities below
$\corlStrongPerfVelocityThreshold$\,m/s, while the single-leg
baseline's performance degrades once commanded velocity exceeds
$\corlVelocityMin$\,m/s and degrades further as terrain difficulty
increases. The performance gap narrows only in the easiest condition
($\corlVelocityMin$\,m/s, flat ground), consistent with concurrent,
high-rate footstep selection becoming more valuable as task difficulty
increases.

\subsection{Emergent Concurrency Analysis}
\label{subsec:results-concurrency}

The central claim of this paper is that multi-leg coordination
emerges from high-rate single-action selection rather than being
planned. \autoref{fig:data-swing-schedule} shows a representative
swing schedule: below a $\corlSwingScheduleTransitionMark$\,s mark, at
a low commanded velocity,
the robot moves one foot at a time with no overlap between swings;
after the commanded velocity increases, the robot begins committing
a second leg while the first is still airborne, without a strict
alternating pattern of any kind. This transition is not triggered by
any explicit mode switch in GaitNet; the same value function and the
same per-tick decision rule govern both regimes, and the increased
concurrency emerges purely from more candidates crossing the
no-action threshold more often as commanded velocity rises.

Swing durations are similarly non-uniform rather than fixed by a
schedule: \autoref{fig:data-swing-duration-histogram} shows a peak
between $\corlSwingHistPeakMin$\,s and $\corlSwingHistPeakMax$\,s for
typical forward motion, with a secondary mode down to
$\corlSwingHistSecondaryMode$\,s used for situational, faster steps
such as recovering a marginal foothold. The mean swing duration
across training settles to $\corlResMeanSwingDuration$\,s with a
standard deviation of $\corlResStdSwingDuration$\,s, indicating the
duration head is in active use rather than collapsing to a constant.

\subsection{Swing Duration Ablation}
\label{subsec:results-duration-ablation}

To assess whether this dynamic duration output is load-bearing, we
revisit an ablation from our prior simulation study
\cite{Sullivan2025}, comparing standard GaitNet against a
Duration-Ablated-GaitNet trained identically but constrained to a
fixed swing duration of $\corlResDurationAblationFixedDuration$\,s,
the approximate mean of the standard model's learned distribution. The
result is a null finding: the duration-ablated model achieves a
$\corlResDurationAblatedSurvivalRate\%$ survival rate, statistically
indistinguishable from the $\corlResGaitNetSurvivalRate\%$ achieved by
the corresponding standard GaitNet variant under matched conditions. We
do not read this as evidence that dynamic
duration is unimportant in general; more plausibly, the tested
terrain and velocity ranges are not demanding enough to expose a
difference, and the reward function does not explicitly incentivize
using the full duration range beyond what is needed to avoid
termination. We report this honestly as a limitation of the present
evaluation rather than removing the duration head from the
architecture, since its cost is negligible and it may prove useful
at higher commanded velocities or on more aggressive terrain than
tested here.
