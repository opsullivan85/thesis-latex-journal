\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\input{fig_overview}

A quadruped crossing broken ground must decide which leg to lift, when,
where to put it down, and how long the swing should last. Most
model-based controllers settle the first two with a fixed gait
\citep{dicarlo_dynamic_2018, ocs2}, which fixes when each leg lifts
regardless of where safe footholds appear. Learned footstep
planners can make all four decisions, and simulation lets them practise
on large amounts of terrain. They are, however, trained on top of one
low-level controller, and a controller does not land the foot exactly
where it is told: each one misses in its own way. On our training
controller the foot lands \corlOvershootSlowMin--\corlOvershootFastMax\,cm past the commanded point, while the
planner keeps footholds only \corlMarginCm\,cm from a hole
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:method-idm}). When a learned planner fails on a new
controller, the fault need not lie in choosing a safe foothold; it can
lie in landing on it.

We therefore treat the controller, from the planner's point of view, as
a closed-loop map from a commanded foothold to a landed one, and ask
what a planner should send so that it can be reused when that map
changes. Our answer separates choosing a step from executing it
(Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). GaitNet chooses, every
\corlControlTickMs\,ms, one step or none among a few sampled safe
footholds per leg, and is not given a gait schedule. An execution layer
then has the controller land the step where and when GaitNet meant. Its
only learned, per-controller part is an inverse footstep model (IDM),
fitted by regression on the controller's own logged steps, that maps
the foothold GaitNet wants to the command that lands the foot there.
For a new controller, the IDM is refitted and GaitNet stays frozen.
This fit is direct inverse modelling \citep{jordan_forward_1992} with
the hindsight relabelling of goal-conditioned reinforcement learning
\citep{andrychowicz_hindsight_2017, ghosh_learning_2021}; it is exact
only under conditions that a stochastic controller violates, which we
make explicit (Sec.~\ref{subsec:idm-theory}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item GaitNet, a footstep planner without a gait schedule that picks
    one step or none per tick from sampled safe footholds, comparing
    the candidates with attention and drawing on a short motion history.
    It extends the GaitNet of \citet{Sullivan2025}, which already chose
    one step per tick with up to two legs in swing, on one controller
    (Secs.~\ref{subsec:method-step}--\ref{subsec:method-network}).
  \item An execution layer whose only per-controller learned part is an
    IDM, with the conditions under which its regression inverts the
    controller's footstep map, its bias otherwise in a linear-Gaussian
    case, and a bound linking its error to the chance of a foot landing
    in a void (Secs.~\ref{subsec:method-idm}
    and~\ref{subsec:idm-theory}).
  \item An evaluation in which GaitNet, trained on a convex MPC in Isaac
    Lab, runs frozen on OCS2's nonlinear MPC and whole-body controller
    with a simulated depth camera, against OCS2's own trot and three
    ablations, with \corlOcsTrialsPerPoint{} trials per point and
    significance tests (Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\end{itemize}
All results are in simulation, on one pair of controllers.

\paragraph{Related work.}
Model-based planners place foothold choice, contact timing and
whole-body motion in one optimization \citep{Wensing2022Nov}; once
contacts are free, the problem becomes mixed-integer. Some methods
restrict footholds to convex regions \citep{deits_footstep_2014,
grandia_perceptive_2022} or
optimize contact schedules and timing together
\citep{winkler_gait_2018}. Gait-free planners choose contact timing
online from per-leg feasibility rules \citep{bjelonic_whole-body_2021}
or by tree search \citep{amatucci_monte_2022}, sped up by a learned
cost-to-go \citep{taouil_non-gaited_2024}, or with learned per-foot
flight phases inside an MPC \citep{patrizi_rl-augmented_2026}.
End-to-end policies decide contacts implicitly in their joint targets
\citep{lee_learning_2020, zhang_learning_2023, fu_minimizing_2021}.
Hybrid methods keep an explicit foothold: RLOC learns it while a gait
fixes the timing \citep{gangapurwala_rloc_2022}, DeepGait
\citep{tsounis_deepgait_2020} also learns which legs are in contact,
\citet{da_learning_2020} learn only which legs are in contact, and
ContactNet
\citep{bratta_contactnet_2024} ranks discretized foothold regions
without prescribing the swing leg, with concurrency and swing timing set
by configuration.

For transfer, \citet{christiano_transfer_2016} keep a policy fixed and
fit an inverse dynamics model on the target system, \citet{xie_fada_2026}
split a humanoid policy into a planner and an inverse dynamics model and
adapt only the latter, and \citet{li_zero-shot_2022} retarget
locomotion policies to new robots through a kinodynamic MPC. At the
footstep level, \citet{akizhanov_learning_2024} correct the contact
targets of an NMPC with a network trained on the contacts it actually
reached, the construction of our IDM, within a fixed gait and one
controller; \citet{taouil_quadrupedal_2023} plan footsteps with learned
motion models of a black-box trotting controller; and
\citet{jenelten_dtc_2024} reuse a learned tracker under new planners. We are not aware of work that
moves a learned, gait-free footstep planner between two model-based
controllers.
