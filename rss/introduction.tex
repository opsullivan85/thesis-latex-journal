\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\input{fig_overview}

Legged robots can cross broken or narrow terrain that stops wheels
and tracks, as long as they find where to place their feet.
Choosing those footholds is only part of the problem: the robot must
also decide which legs to move, when to lift them, and how long their swings
should last. Together, these decisions are called
\emph{contact planning}. Most controllers simplify contact planning
with a fixed gait \cite{dicarlo_dynamic_2018, ocs2}, which sets the leg
timing and ordering in advance. This restricts the motions available to
the robot, and a fixed schedule cannot react when safe footholds appear
at irregular places and times. Without a gait, a planner can instead
produce \emph{acyclic} contact sequences, which follow neither a gait
cycle nor a gait library; the literature also calls them non-gaited or
free-gait.

Learned footstep planners can make these choices implicitly, and
simulation lets them practice on large amounts of terrain. A learned
footstep planner, however, is trained on top of one low-level
controller, and if it is deployed on another, it meets a different
mapping from command to landing: a controller does not always place the
foot where it is told, and each controller misses in its own way. Even
a miss of a few centimeters can exceed the safety margin a planner keeps
around terrain hazards, and a new controller may also start each step
on its own schedule. When a learned footstep planner fails on a new
controller, the fault therefore need not lie in choosing a safe
foothold; it can lie in landing on it.

Model-based planners place
foothold choice, contact timing, and whole-body motion inside one
model predictive controller \cite{Wensing2022Nov}. This works well
when the contact pattern is known, but once the planner must choose
contacts, the problem becomes a mixed-integer program. Some methods
reduce the search by limiting footholds to convex terrain regions
\cite{deits_footstep_2014}, and others optimize contact schedules and
timing together \cite{winkler_gait_2018}. Gait-free planners remove the
periodic schedule altogether, choosing contact timing online from

per-leg feasibility rules \cite{bjelonic_whole-body_2021} or by tree

Learning-based methods take a different route. End-to-end policies
make the contact decision inside one neural controller and cross hard
terrain reliably \cite{lee_learning_2020, zhang_learning_2023}; trained
without an explicit schedule, they can even develop gaits on their own
\cite{fu_minimizing_2021}. Their contact decision, however, is only
implicit in the joint targets, so it cannot be handed to a model-based
controller. Other methods keep an explicit foothold. Perceptive
model-based stacks project it onto a convex terrain region
\cite{grandia_perceptive_2022}, and hybrid methods learn it while a gait
generator still fixes the timing
\cite{gangapurwala_rloc_2022, tsounis_deepgait_2020}. The closest
work, ContactNet \cite{bratta_contactnet_2024}, ranks discretized
foothold candidates with a network without prescribing which leg
swings. Its concurrency, however, is set by configuration, it re-ranks
candidates only at touchdown, and its gait sets the swing timing.

Finally, work on moving learned policies to new robots or domains has
moved body motion, joint actions, or footholds within a fixed gait,
but not gait-free footstep choices. Li et al.
\cite{li_zero-shot_2022} retarget learned quadruped locomotion
policies to new robots through a hybrid kinodynamic MPC, and RLOC
\cite{gangapurwala_rloc_2022} moves its learned footstep policy to a
larger robot while keeping its gait. Xie et al. \cite{xie_fada_2026}
split a humanoid policy into a planner, which states the intended
motion, and an inverse dynamics model, which turns it into actions,
and then adapt only the inverse model to a new domain.

We present GaitNet (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}), which separates choosing
a step from executing it. At every control tick, the planner picks one
step, or no step, from a few safe candidate footholds per leg, kept
away from voids, the holes in the ground. An execution layer then has
the controller land each step where and when the planner meant. Its
core is an inverse footstep model (IDM, named after the inverse
dynamics model of \cite{xie_fada_2026}), fitted from the controller's
own logged steps, which turns where GaitNet wants the foot into the
command that lands it there. GaitNet is trained in Isaac Lab
\cite{mittal_orbit_2023} with the IDM of its training controller in the loop,
and then runs frozen on OCS2 \cite{ocs2}, a nonlinear model
predictive controller (NMPC) with whole-body control (WBC); only the
IDM is refit, on OCS2's own logged steps, and steps are delivered on
OCS2's own schedule. GaitNet builds on the one-step-per-tick action
space of \cite{Sullivan2025}, tested there on one controller, and adds
candidate attention, a motion history, and the execution layer. All
results are in simulation.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item GaitNet, a footstep planner that is given no gait. Every tick,
    it picks one step or none from sampled safe footholds, comparing
    the candidates with attention and drawing on a short motion history
    (Secs.~\ref{subsec:method-step} and~\ref{subsec:method-network}).
  \item An execution layer that lets the frozen GaitNet run on a new
    controller, with an inverse footstep model per controller fitted by
    hindsight relabelling from that controller's own logged steps
    (Secs.~\ref{subsec:method-idm} and~\ref{subsec:method-deployment}).
  \item An evaluation on OCS2 with a simulated depth camera, over
    0--\corlOcsMaxVoidPct\% voids and
    \corlOcsSpeedMin--\corlOcsSpeedMax\,m/s, in which GaitNet matches
    OCS2's own trot on sparse voids and exceeds it on dense voids and
    at faster commands (Sec.~\ref{subsec:exp-transfer}).
  \item Ablations of overlapping swings, the IDM, and the candidate
    comparison (Sec.~\ref{subsec:exp-why}).
\end{itemize}
