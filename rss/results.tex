\section{Simulation Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We ask three questions. (Q1)~Does the frozen GaitNet outperform OCS2's own trot on OCS2 as
voids get denser and commands faster?
(Q2)~Does GaitNet benefit from swinging more than one leg at a time?
(Q3)~How much do the IDM and the comparison between candidates
contribute?

\subsection{Setup}
\label{subsec:exp-setup}

We run GaitNet on OCS2 in Gazebo (Sec.~\ref{subsec:method-deployment}),
with the IDM fitted on OCS2's own steps (Sec.~\ref{subsec:method-idm}).
All GaitNet variants use one checkpoint, iteration
\verify{\corlOcsCheckpoint} of a seeded run trained from scratch with the
IDM in the loop (Sec.~\ref{subsec:method-training}), frozen after
training. Each world is a strip of
pillars in which each tile is a real drop with probability equal to
the void fraction. We test eight void fractions, from 0 to
\corlOcsMaxVoidPct\%, and five forward speeds, from \corlOcsSpeedMin{}
to \corlOcsSpeedMax\,m/s, with \corlOcsTrialsPerCell{} trials for each
pair, and 14 for OCS2's own trot in the comparison by speed. The
world seeds are new, disjoint from those that tuned the execution
layer, supplied the IDM's data, or were used to develop the camera
pipeline. The fastest command lies above the \corlCmdVxMax\,m/s that
GaitNet was trained on, and void fractions above \corlMaxVoidPct\% lie
above those it was trained on. The tiles are larger than the training
cells (\corlGzTileCm{} against \corlTerrainCellCm\,cm), so the two void
fractions are not directly comparable. A trial succeeds if the robot reaches the end
of a \corlOcsTrackM\,m track without falling or leaving the strip,
within \corlOcsTimeFactor{} times the time the command needs, from
90\,s at \corlOcsSpeedMin\,m/s to 18\,s at \corlOcsSpeedMax\,m/s.

We compare five methods. Every method is tested on the same terrain,
with the same simulated depth pipeline, and runs on the
same NMPC and WBC. The methods differ only in how steps are chosen
and executed:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item GaitNet: as deployed; up to two legs may swing at once.
  \item OCS2's own trot: OCS2's perceptive trot, with its own gait
    scheduler and footholds.
  \item GaitNet-Single: the same weights, but only one leg may swing at
    a time.
  \item GaitNet, no IDM: the same weights, but the chosen landing point
    is sent to OCS2 unchanged.
  \item Per-candidate + IDM: the per-candidate network of
    \cite{Sullivan2025} (Sec.~\ref{subsec:method-network}), which scores
    each candidate on its own and has no history. It is trained with
    GaitNet's recipe and the IDM in the loop, and runs with the same
    OCS2 IDM; we show the first of several seeded runs, chosen before
    evaluation.
\end{enumerate}
With 50 trials per void fraction, a success rate between 30 and 70\%
carries a 95\% interval of about $\pm$13 points, so we read only
larger or consistent differences as real.

\subsection{Comparison with OCS2's Own Trot}
\label{subsec:exp-transfer}

GaitNet matches OCS2's own trot on sparse voids and gains a lead as the
voids get denser (Fig.~\ref{fig:ocs2-results}a). Both succeed in every
trial up to 10\% voids and are within two points at 20\%. From 30\%
voids on, GaitNet leads at every void fraction, with
\corlOcsDenseFull\% against \corlOcsDenseTrot\% success over
40--\corlOcsMaxVoidPct\% voids. The gap is largest from 50\% voids on,
beyond the densest voids GaitNet was trained on: there it still
succeeds in 50--80\% of the trials, and at \corlOcsMaxVoidPct\% voids
in half of them, against few for OCS2's own trot.

Because both see the same terrain and run on the same tracker, this
gap comes from how the steps and gait are chosen. OCS2's trot lifts each diagonal
pair on a fixed schedule and must then find a foothold for it, while
GaitNet chooses at every tick which leg to move, if any, and where.
On sparse voids, a safe foothold is almost always close to where the
trot would step, so the fixed schedule costs nothing. On dense voids,
safe cells are fewer and further apart; our interpretation, which
these trials do not test directly, is that the trot is then often
forced to step when no good foothold is in reach, whereas GaitNet can
wait, move another leg first, or take a longer step to a safe cell.

GaitNet also leads at every commanded speed
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ocs2-results}b), by 5 to 17 points. Both lose success
as the command rises, and both lose most at \corlOcsSpeedMax\,m/s,
above the range GaitNet was trained on, yet GaitNet still succeeds in
two thirds of those trials. In the trials that succeed, GaitNet also
walks closer to the command, at 89--94\% of it against 78--87\% for
OCS2's own trot. Because this speed is averaged only over successful
trials, it describes how well each method tracks the command when it
gets through, not how far it walks on average.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{subsec:exp-why}

Overlapping swings matter (Q2). Under a one-swing limit, GaitNet still
walks, but it succeeds less often at every void fraction and every
speed, \corlOcsAllSingle\% against \corlOcsAllFull\% on average over
void fractions. The gap grows with speed, from 10 points at
\corlOcsSpeedMin\,m/s to more than 20 points from 0.10\,m/s on, and in
its successful trials GaitNet-Single falls further behind the command
at the higher speeds. It also takes shorter steps:
\corlOcsStepsPerMSingle{} steps per metre against
\corlOcsStepsPerMFull{} for GaitNet. With one leg in the air at a time
and swings of at least \corlSwingDurationMin\,s, it cannot step more
than about ten times per second, yet at \corlOcsSpeedMax\,m/s, with
steps this short, it would need about eleven. This is the likely
reason it falls behind, and fails more often, as the command rises. Because GaitNet was never trained under this
limit, these results show that GaitNet relies on occasionally swinging
two legs together, not that overlap is better in general.

The IDM contributes most (Q3). Without it, GaitNet's success falls from
\corlOcsAllFull\% to \corlOcsAllNoIdm\% on average over void
fractions, below GaitNet-Single at most void fractions. Only on ground
without voids, where a missed landing costs little, does GaitNet do
well without it; already at 10\% voids it loses 20 points. GaitNet
chooses cells that are safe with a margin of \corlMarginCm\,cm. Without the IDM, OCS2 lands the foot
\corlOcsLandRmsNoIdm\,cm RMS from the chosen cell, three times that
margin, so a foot aimed next to a void often ends up in it; with the
IDM, the error drops to \corlOcsLandRmsIdm\,cm, about the margin itself,
in a separate test on flat ground \verify{(speeds to be stated)}. On OCS2,
landing each step where it was chosen thus matters more than being
able to overlap swings.

Comparing candidates matters on dense voids. The per-candidate network
was trained with the same recipe and runs with the same IDM, but
scores each candidate on its own and has no motion history. It is
about as good as GaitNet up to 20\% voids but falls behind from 30\%
on, with \corlOcsDenseMlp\% against \corlOcsDenseFull\% over
40--\corlOcsMaxVoidPct\% voids. The gap is largest at 30 and 40\%
voids and shrinks at the densest voids, where both often fail. The
comparison changes both the candidate mixer and the history at once,
and each network is a single run, so it does not show which of the
two matters more.
