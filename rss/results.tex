\section{Simulation Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We ask five questions. (Q1)~Does the frozen GaitNet succeed on OCS2 as
often as OCS2's own trot? (Q2)~Which leg coordination emerges when no
gait is given, and does GaitNet need to pick leg pairs freely?
(Q3)~Does the transfer depend on the choice of step or on its
execution? (Q4)~Does GaitNet still work when terrain comes from a
depth camera? (Q5)~How does GaitNet cope with denser voids, and how
repeatable is its training?

\subsection{Training and Evaluation Setup}
\label{subsec:exp-setup}

All OCS2 conditions use one GaitNet checkpoint, iteration
\corlTrainingIterations{} of one training run, frozen after training.
\pending{Which IDM each OCS2 result uses will be stated with the
updated results.} The run's settings changed while it trained. It used
omnidirectional commands for its first \corlOmniIterations{}
iterations, during which the curriculum was changed twice and its
state was reset at each restart; one segment ran on to iteration 1103 % TODO-macro gaitnet-docs/runs.md (SC-scratch-fast-r2 ran to 1103)
and was rolled back to its best checkpoint, at \corlOmniIterations,
which is also one of the IDM's two data sources. The run then trained
on forward commands without the IDM until iteration
\corlIdmStartIteration, and with the IDM in the loop after that. It
was not seeded and used $\corlTotalTransitions$ transitions, about
\corlTrainHours\,h on one NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000.

On the training controller, we also evaluate three other policies.
GaitNet (eval IDM) shares GaitNet's run up to iteration
\corlIdmStartIteration, then continued without the IDM and saw it only
at evaluation. The two per-candidate baselines use the network of
\cite{Sullivan2025} (Sec.~\ref{subsec:method-network}).
\verify{Per-candidate (prior) was trained before this work, with the
preliminary version's controller, commands, and curriculum}, on a CPU
version of our training controller. Per-candidate (ours) is fine-tuned
from it with our rewards, curriculum, and controller randomization, but
with longer rollouts.

Isaac Lab tests run on the training controller without randomization,
with one void fraction and one forward speed, \corlSimSpeedSlow{} or
\corlSimSpeedFast\,m/s, per test \emph{cell}, with
\corlSimRobotsPerCell{} robots for \corlSimEpisodeDuration\,s. Every
policy takes its top-scoring choice, and because terrain is generated
per cell, we compare policies only within one evaluation. A trial
succeeds if the robot does not fail and walks at least half the
commanded distance; at \corlSimSpeedSlow\,m/s, that is 1\,m, of which % TODO-macro experiment-setup.md 1 (strip, spawn platform)
the first 0.5\,m is the solid spawn platform. The \emph{10--30\% band}
averages the 10, 20, and 30\% void cells.

\subsection{OCS2 Terrain Benchmark}
\label{subsec:exp-transfer}

We run the frozen GaitNet on OCS2 (Sec.~\ref{subsec:method-deployment})
and compare it with OCS2's own trot \cite{ocs2, leggedcontrol} (Q1).
Each world is a strip of pillars in which each tile is a real drop
with probability equal to the void fraction. The tiles are larger than
the training cells (\corlGzTileCm{} against \corlTerrainCellCm\,cm)
because the camera pipeline cannot see smaller voids, so void
fractions in the two simulators are not comparable. Each world seed
has one fixed role: seeds 51--60 tuned the execution layer, seeds
61--110 supplied OCS2 steps for an OCS2-specific IDM, and seeds 1--20
are the held-out benchmark. However, the camera pipeline, including
OCS2's estimator slip gate, was developed on seeds 1--40, which
include them.

Each condition runs \corlGzTrialsPerArm{} trials of
\corlGzTrialSeconds\,s at \corlGzSpeed\,m/s on the held-out seeds at
10, 20, and 30\% voids. A trial succeeds under the same rule as in
Isaac Lab; the failure limits are measured from the surface, and
leaving the strip also counts as a failure. Rates carry 95\% Wilson
score intervals, and we compare trial counts with two-sided Fisher
exact tests.

OCS2's own trot uses its native gait scheduler with fixed swings and
picks its own footholds on the same terrain map. The GaitNet
conditions keep OCS2's NMPC and WBC but replace its gait scheduler and
foothold choice with GaitNet's steps, delivered through the execution
layer, so the comparison focuses on the planner rather than the
tracker. We evaluate four conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item OCS2's own trot: OCS2's scheduled trot, no GaitNet.
  \item GaitNet-Single: only one leg may swing at a time.
  \item GaitNet-Trot: only diagonal leg pairs may swing
    together, as in a conventional trot.
  \item GaitNet (Full): GaitNet as deployed; up to two legs
    may swing at once, in any pair.
\end{enumerate}
The three GaitNet variants share the same weights and differ only in
the concurrency constraints applied during action selection.

\subsection{Concurrency Ablation}
\label{subsec:exp-coordination}

GaitNet-Single, GaitNet-Trot, and GaitNet (Full) share the same
weights and differ only in which second swing is allowed, so their
difference isolates the role of overlapping swings (Q2). We report the
step rate, the swing duration, the mean number of feet in the air, the
share of no-step choices, and which leg pairs swing together. The gait
statistics in Table~\ref{tab:gazebo-gaits} come from the true foot
heights, measured the same way for every condition, so measured swings
are shorter than scheduled ones.

Although it is given no gait, GaitNet coordinates its legs like a
trot. It mostly swings one leg at a time: it takes fewer and shorter
steps than OCS2's own trot, keeps \xx{} feet in the air on average,
and chooses the no-step option on \xx\% of ticks. When it does swing
two legs together, they are almost always a diagonal pair: of its
paired swings, \xx{} were diagonal, \xx{} were the two rear legs,
\xx{} were on one side, and \xx{} were the two front legs. This
pattern comes from repeated decisions of the same network and no-step
comparison, together with the two feasibility rules
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:method-step}), not from a schedule. Restricting
GaitNet to diagonal pairs (GaitNet-Trot) gives \xx\% success, so under
these conditions the freedom to pick any pair does not raise success.

GaitNet cannot, however, walk one leg at a time. GaitNet-Single
succeeds in \xx\% of trials ($p < \xx$ against GaitNet (Full)), and
the robot tips over within seconds in nearly every trial. Because
GaitNet was never trained under a one-swing limit, this shows that
GaitNet depends on occasionally swinging two legs together, not that
overlap is better in general.

\subsection{Negative Results}
\label{subsec:exp-negative}

Several ideas did not help. Sampling the action, as in training, was
worse than taking the top-scoring choice for every policy. On OCS2, no
swing or posture weight we tried brought the landing scatter down;
more SQP iterations, a shorter MPC horizon, a longer lead before each
step, and a constant per-leg landing correction gave no gain; and our % TODO-macro gaitnet-docs/results.md sec. 6 (item 3)
earlier changes to OCS2's task settings broke its own gait at
0.15--0.2\,m/s, so we restored six of them to their stock values.
Training with a broadly randomized controller, meant to cover
OCS2-like controllers, made policies robust on flat ground on an
\emph{OCS2-like executor}, the training controller with landing
offsets, landing noise, and a lift-off delay set to mimic how OCS2
executed GaitNet's steps. It also cost them their skill on voids on the
training controller; one fell from \xx{} to \xx\% on 10--30\% voids at
the slower speed. On the OCS2-like executor, the per-candidate network
was also more robust on flat ground than GaitNet (\xx--\xx\% against
\xx--\xx\%). A privileged teacher reached \xx\% on 10--30\% voids but
did not transfer to other controllers, so we did not distill it.
